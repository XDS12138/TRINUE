### 摘要
Underwater image enhancement is a critical component for ensuring the reliability of underwater visual tasks. However, existing methods often struggle to simultaneously restore accurate color and fine structural details, especially under complex degradations caused by scattering and refraction, such as blurring, texture loss, and geometric distortion. To address these challenges, this paper proposes a novel deep learning framework, UnderwaterEnhanceNet, which adopts a dual-task-driven architecture that explicitly models color shifts (low-frequency information) and structural degradation (high-frequency information), enabling coordinated restoration of both color and detail. At the core of the framework lies an adaptive depth fusion module, which leverages scene depth to dynamically regulate multi-scale features, thereby achieving physically-aware, region-specific enhancement.

To overcome the limitations of real-world data scarcity, we construct a large-scale controllable synthetic underwater dataset based on an optical propagation model. Using 3D simulation and ray tracing, we generate paired images with realistic color distortions, structural blurring, and high-precision depth maps, providing high-quality supervision during training. The network employs a Transformer-based backbone integrated with multi-scale skip connections and feature fusion modules, ensuring both semantic coherence and fine-detail recovery. During training, the model is jointly optimized using raw images, depth maps, and corresponding clean references; during inference, only a single raw image is required for fully automatic enhancement. Experimental results on multiple benchmark underwater datasets demonstrate that the proposed method significantly outperforms existing approaches, exhibiting strong generalization and superior structure-preserving capability.


## 1. Introduction

Underwater image acquisition remains a persistent challenge in computer vision due to the highly dynamic and non-uniform optical properties of water. The underwater environment introduces complex degradations that arise primarily from wavelength-dependent light absorption and scattering, significantly altering both the photometric and structural fidelity of captured images. As light travels through water, longer wavelengths (e.g., red and orange) attenuate more rapidly than shorter wavelengths (e.g., blue and green), resulting in strong color casts and diminished contrast \[1]. Scattering by water molecules and suspended particulates further degrades image quality by reducing sharpness and increasing haze, particularly in regions with increased depth or turbidity \[2]. These degradations not only impair visual perception but also hinder the performance of downstream tasks such as underwater navigation, mapping, object detection, and ecological monitoring \[3].

### 1.1 Limitations of Traditional Approaches

Early efforts to mitigate these degradations focused on physically interpretable models of underwater light propagation. Classical model-based methods, such as those adapted from the Dark Channel Prior (DCP) \[4], Red Channel Prior \[17], or the Underwater DCP (UDCP) \[5], estimate transmission maps and background light to perform inverse rendering of the scene. While these approaches yield promising results under idealized conditions, they are sensitive to inaccuracies in depth estimation, assumptions about uniform illumination, and the physical homogeneity of water properties, making them fragile in real-world settings.

Alternatively, image processing-based techniques—such as histogram equalization \[6], multiscale Retinex \[8], and wavelet-based fusion \[7]—directly manipulate pixel-level statistics or perform transform-domain adjustments to enhance contrast or correct color balance. However, such methods often operate without a physical understanding of underwater degradation and can inadvertently amplify noise, over-enhance colors, or destroy fine texture, especially in scenes with spatially varying depth and turbidity.

### 1.2 The Rise of Learning-Based Methods

With the proliferation of deep learning, data-driven approaches have revolutionized the field of image enhancement by learning end-to-end mappings from degraded to restored images. Generative models such as WaterGAN \[10] and FUnIE-GAN \[18] pioneered the use of adversarial training to produce perceptually realistic underwater restorations. These methods demonstrated that neural networks can implicitly learn to disentangle color casts, contrast loss, and blurring, provided sufficient supervision and representative datasets.

Subsequent advances introduced physics-aware and hybrid architectures. Water-Net \[11] integrates underwater priors into its design to guide feature learning, while UWCNN \[12] employs a residual CNN with tailored constraints to regularize underwater enhancement. More recent efforts explore stronger architectural expressiveness: PUGAN \[13] couples a physical image formation model with GAN training, and U-Shape Transformer \[14] leverages long-range dependencies via windowed attention to model global structure. Diffusion-based methods such as Osmosis (ECCV 2024) have also shown potential in iterative refinement of underwater textures.

Despite their impressive progress, current methods face two key limitations. First, **spatially varying degradation** is often treated uniformly across the image, overlooking the strong depth-dependent nature of light attenuation and scattering. Second, while several works incorporate sophisticated loss functions (e.g., SSIM, perceptual losses), they often lack a unified, physically motivated strategy to simultaneously preserve structural fidelity, perceptual quality, and frequency-domain information.

### 1.3 Our Approach

To address these challenges, we introduce **UnderwaterEnhanceNet**, a novel deep learning framework for underwater image enhancement that explicitly leverages scene depth as an adaptive guidance signal. Instead of treating depth as a static prior or a passive input, we design a depth-aware modulation mechanism that dynamically influences the internal feature representations of the network across scales.

The core hypothesis of UnderwaterEnhanceNet is that **depth serves as a latent controller** for the spatial variation of degradation. Our architecture incorporates an **adaptive depth fusion module** that injects learned, depth-conditioned fusion weights into the encoder-decoder pathway, allowing the network to adjust its enhancement strategy based on the severity of local degradation. In regions where color casts are strong due to depth-induced attenuation, the network emphasizes color recovery, while in shallow regions, it focuses more on contrast and detail restoration. This **spatially adaptive restoration** is key to handling the diversity and heterogeneity of real-world underwater environments.

UnderwaterEnhanceNet adopts a **hierarchical multi-level encoder-decoder architecture** composed of lightweight bottleneck residual blocks and (optionally) attention heads to balance computational efficiency and expressive power. The use of attention, inspired by recent Transformer-based restoration models such as Restormer and U-Shape Transformer \[14], facilitates the capture of long-range contextual information, which is critical for restoring globally coherent color and texture patterns.

Training is guided by a **comprehensive hybrid loss function** that optimizes for fidelity across multiple domains. This includes:

* **Pixel-level consistency**, via L1 loss;
* **Structural similarity**, via SSIM;
* **Perceptual realism**, through deep feature loss \[16];
* **Frequency alignment**, via FFT-domain regularization;
* **Gradient preservation**, ensuring edge and texture sharpness;
* **Depth consistency**, encouraging coherent restorations across depth transitions;
* **Teacher-student distillation**, using a self-supervised paradigm to enhance generalization without increasing inference cost.

This hybrid loss framework is specifically designed to prevent common pitfalls of enhancement networks—such as color over-correction, edge smoothing, or artifact hallucination—by jointly supervising the network from low-level pixel signals to high-level semantic features.

Moreover, the entire framework is equipped with an advanced visualization system that tracks the evolution of internal features, depth fusion weights, and performance metrics via TensorBoard, enabling detailed interpretability and model debugging.

### 1.4 Contributions

This paper makes the following key contributions:

1. **A novel depth-adaptive fusion mechanism** that dynamically modulates multi-scale features based on depth, enabling spatially-varying enhancement tailored to the severity of local degradation.

2. **An expressive multi-level encoder-decoder backbone**, enhanced with residual bottlenecks and optional attention layers, allowing effective restoration of color, structure, and texture across diverse underwater scenes.

3. **A unified hybrid loss function** that integrates spatial, frequency, perceptual, and structural objectives, guided by a teacher-student distillation framework to improve robustness and generalization.

4. **Extensive experimental validation** on multiple benchmark datasets, demonstrating that UnderwaterEnhanceNet consistently outperforms recent state-of-the-art methods, including CNN, GAN, and Transformer-based approaches, in both qualitative and quantitative evaluations.

# Related Work

## Physical Model-Based Underwater Restoration

Early approaches tackle underwater image degradation by inverting a **physical imaging model** of light attenuation and scattering. These methods assume an underwater version of the atmospheric haze model, where scene radiance is exponentially attenuated with distance and overlaid by **veiling light** (backscatter).  By estimating parameters like the transmission (depth-dependent light decay) and ambient waterlight, these methods attempt to recover a clear image. A prominent example is the adaptation of the **Dark Channel Prior (DCP)** to underwater scenes. DCP, originally developed for terrestrial haze removal, assumes that in any local patch the minimum intensity in at least one color channel is very low, which allows estimation of haze thickness. Underwater images violate standard DCP assumptions because the ambient illumination is non-white (often bluish-green) and the red channel is heavily attenuated. **Underwater DCP (UDCP)** methods modify the prior by excluding the red channel or using only the blue-green channels when computing the dark channel. This helps estimate a **depth (transmission) map** for the scene, which is then used to correct color cast and enhance contrast. Chiang and Chen’s pioneering work combined wavelength compensation with a dark-channel dehazing step, and later Drews *et al.* refined UDCP to better handle the non-uniform ambient light underwater. The core assumption is that some patch of the scene is naturally almost-black after heavy attenuation, which may fail in brightly lit or turbidity-dominated scenes. Indeed, **non-uniform lighting or lack of truly dark regions can violate the dark channel prior**, leading to depth map errors. This often causes **halo artifacts or under/over-correction** in the restored image. Recent variants introduce regularization (e.g. total variation) to smooth the transmission map or multi-scale priors to mitigate halos, but they still struggle if the image contains strong foreground illumination or if water properties vary across the scene.

In parallel, the **Red Channel Prior** was proposed to specifically address the rapid attenuation of long wavelengths (red light). Galdran *et al.* observe that distant underwater regions lack red intensity, and thus boosting the red channel (while suppressing excessive blue/green) can recover a more balanced color. Their **Automatic Red Channel** method treats the red color restoration as an analogue to DCP (operating on the “dark” red channel). By assuming that objects at distance *should* reflect some red (which has been lost due to absorption), the method adds red content until a physical plausibility constraint is met. This prior gracefully handles scenes with artificial lighting where DCP fails, yielding more natural colors in many cases. However, a limitation is that aggressively amplifying a near-zero red channel can also amplify noise and may **overshoot, producing a reddish hue** if the water had very little red to begin with. Thus, red-channel methods often require careful balancing or post-processing to avoid new color cast artifacts. Other physics-based restorations build on the **Beer-Lambert law** and scattering theory. For example, Peng and Cosman’s **Blurriness and Light Absorption (IBLA)** method estimates scene depth by analyzing image blur and color loss. The idea is that distant objects not only lose red color but also appear more blurred due to scattering. IBLA computes a depth map from those cues and plugs it into the formation model to recover the image. This approach assumes that focus blur correlates with distance, which may break down if the camera’s focus or aperture is not uniform, or in presence of motion blur. Notably, Peng *et al.* report that **lighting variations often invalidate simpler priors** like DCP or “maximum intensity” (bright channel) methods, leading to poor results. By contrast, IBLA’s depth estimation proved more robust under varying illumination. Still, its performance can degrade if the scene contains both very clear regions and very turbid regions that violate the one-to-one mapping between blur and distance. Other model-based methods incorporate prior knowledge of water properties: e.g., **prioritizing color lines** or enforcing spectral consistency. Many require the user to input the type of water (diffuse attenuation coefficients) or assume a single homogeneous water type across the image. If these parameters are mis-specified, the methods can **over-correct (introducing an over-saturated appearance) or under-correct (leaving residual haze)**. In summary, physical-model approaches ground the enhancement in optics and often generalize across scenes better than naive image processing, but they are **limited by their strong assumptions**. Typical failure modes include **incorrect depth estimation** (hence incorrect color compensation) when no suitable dark/red pixels are present, and **amplification of noise or introduction of color tint** when pushing the model beyond its valid range. Some methods also tend to **lose fine details** by over-smoothing during color correction, as the algorithms focus on global contrast and color fidelity over texture.

## Image Processing Enhancement Techniques

A large body of work improves underwater images using general image processing algorithms **without explicitly modeling the underwater physics**. These techniques treat the symptoms (low contrast, color cast) via pixel intensity remapping, filtering, and fusion. A straightforward approach is **global contrast stretching or histogram equalization (HE)** applied to each color channel. Histogram equalization spreads out the pixel value distribution, attempting to use the full available range of intensities. In underwater scenes, this can indeed brighten a dark blue-green image and increase contrast. However, because one channel (red) may be mostly empty (zero values at distance), naive HE can **result in severe color distortion** – for instance, amplifying camera noise in the red channel or making the water background unnaturally yellow once equalized. Variants like **CLAHE (Contrast Limited Adaptive HE)** perform local equalization with limits to prevent over-amplification of noise, yielding better results than global HE on many underwater photos. Yet, even CLAHE cannot recover true colors; it often produces a flat-looking image with a uniform haze color, since it has no concept of depth-dependent attenuation. Another common step is **color balancing or white-balancing**, which adjusts the image’s overall RGB gains to neutralize the color cast. Assuming the average scene illumination should be gray (gray-world assumption) or using a reference white object if available, these methods boost the red channel and sometimes reduce blue/green. White balance is fast and can greatly improve perceptual color balance, but it too is global – it cannot distinguish a **blue object under shallow water** from a **white object viewed through deep water**, potentially leading to over-compensation. For example, white-balancing an image taken under pure blue ambient light may yield a noisy red tint in truly distant regions.

Beyond global adjustments, researchers have applied **Retinex-based enhancement** to underwater images. Retinex algorithms (single-scale or multi-scale) assume an image is the product of reflectance and illumination. By estimating and dividing out the smooth illumination component, one enhances the reflectance (intrinsic colors and details). Underwater, a Retinex approach can correct non-uniform illumination and sharpen details in dark regions. However, the **physical meaning of “illumination” in underwater scenes is different** – much of the color distortion is due to wavelength-specific absorption rather than a smooth shading. Thus, Retinex tends to only address part of the problem (e.g., it might improve contrast in shaded areas but not restore missing colors). Moreover, if not carefully constrained, Retinex enhancement can produce **halo artifacts around edges** or **amplify background noise**, because it boosts local contrast without understanding the global haze. Researchers have proposed hybrid methods, for instance combining Retinex with color correction or denoising to mitigate these issues.

Another influential line of work is **multi-image fusion from a single exposure**, exemplified by Ancuti *et al.’s* fusion method. This technique derives two or more modified versions of the original image – typically, one color-corrected image (to fix global color cast) and one contrast-enhanced image (e.g., by histogram stretching or sharpening) – and then fuses them using weight maps that favor well-exposed, high-contrast, and color-balanced regions. The fusion process is designed to **leverage the strengths of each input**: for instance, regions where the original image is very hazy may get most of their information from the contrast-enhanced version, whereas regions with good visibility but slight color tint might primarily come from the white-balanced version. This approach does not explicitly compute any physical parameters; instead it **heuristically improves visibility**. Fusion methods can significantly increase the clarity of distant objects and produce an appealing result without requiring calibration or multiple images. They also typically include steps to avoid amplifying noise (e.g., edge-preserving smoothing during fusion). Nonetheless, their limitations become evident in extreme conditions: if an entire image is very poor (e.g., dense greenish fog), all fused inputs are similarly poor, so fusion cannot invent details or true color. Also, fusion weight maps are usually based on local contrast and color saturation; this may **fail for scenes with, say, a uniformly colored object of interest** – the object might be given low weight if it lacks texture, causing its color to wash out in the fusion result. In general, purely image-based enhancements often **struggle with the balance between amplifying signal and amplifying noise**. They assume that what’s missing can be compensated by reallocation of pixel values, but when information is truly lost (e.g., no red wavelengths returned from a far object), these methods **cannot recover that content** – the best they can do is a plausible guess or a subjective improvement in visibility. Typical failure modes include **oversaturation** (unnaturally vivid colors, especially if the method overestimates the needed correction) and **spatial inconsistencies** (some regions look more enhanced than others, since there’s no global model of degradation). Despite these issues, image processing techniques remain popular for quick-and-effective enhancement, and they are often used as preprocessing steps. In fact, some modern learning methods incorporate these techniques (e.g. white balance, CLAHE) as pre-processing or augmentation to generate training data or multi-stream inputs. This highlights both their utility and their insufficiency if used alone – they set a baseline that more advanced methods seek to surpass.

## Deep Learning-Based Enhancement (CNNs, GANs, Transformers,Diffusion)

With the rise of deep learning, numerous data-driven approaches have been proposed for underwater image enhancement. These methods aim to learn the complex nonlinear mapping from a raw underwater image to a “restored” image, using large datasets for supervision. Early deep models were often based on **Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)** with architectures adapted from general image-to-image translation (such as encoder–decoder networks or residual CNNs). A key challenge for learning-based UIE (Underwater Image Enhancement) is the lack of ground-truth reference images – one cannot easily obtain the true clear scene for a given underwater image. Researchers have addressed this by creating **synthetic paired datasets** or by using **reference-enhanced images** as proxies (more on data in a later section). For example, Li *et al.* built the **UIEB benchmark** with 950 real underwater images each paired with a **human-chosen “best” enhanced version**. Using this dataset, they trained a CNN called **Water-Net** as a baseline enhancement model. Water-Net introduced a novel **gated multi-stream architecture**: instead of feeding only the raw image, it takes multiple versions of the image processed by simple algorithms – specifically a white-balanced version, a histogram-equalized version, and a gamma-corrected version – and learns to fuse them. The network produces spatial **confidence maps** for each of the three inputs, indicating where each enhancement is most useful, and blends the inputs accordingly. This design was motivated by the observation that no single preprocessing works for the entire image (for instance, gamma correction might help in dark areas but over-brighten already well-lit areas). Water-Net’s fused output outperformed any single enhancement method, demonstrating the power of learnable fusion. However, its **assumption** is that the types of degradation can be addressed by those three fixed preprocessing techniques; if an image has, say, a severe blurring from scatter that none of the three inputs can recover, Water-Net cannot invent it. Furthermore, Water-Net (and most CNN models) are usually trained on data from certain water conditions – they may **generalize poorly to very different water types**. Li *et al.* noted that no single model handled all water types well, which led them to an interesting strategy in a later work: training **multiple specialized CNNs for different water conditions**. Their **UWCNN** model family trains separate networks for several discrete water types (clear ocean, turbid green, etc.), using a synthetic data generator to produce training pairs for each type. At runtime, one would classify the input image’s water type and then apply the corresponding CNN. This yields better color fidelity for each category but requires **knowing the water type a priori** and maintaining many sub-models. It also underscores a limitation of many learning methods: **distribution shift** – if the real image falls outside the training distribution, the network may fail spectacularly (e.g., producing unnatural colors or artifacts). Some works attempt to incorporate physics into CNNs to improve robustness. For instance, **Ucolor** (Li *et al.*) integrates an estimated transmission map into a deep model’s input and uses multi-color-space learning, bridging model-based and learning-based methods. These hybrid designs can improve generalization by giving the network explicit physical cues. Still, purely CNN-based approaches often focus on per-pixel fidelity (L1/L2 losses against ground truth) and can **struggle with perceptual quality** – sometimes yielding overly smooth or slightly blurry results that minimize error but are not crisply restored.

To address perceptual quality, Generative Adversarial Networks (**GANs**) have been employed for UIE. GAN-based models introduce a discriminator that critiques the enhanced images, pushing the generator (the enhancement network) to produce more realistic and visually appealing outputs. One notable example is **FUnIE-GAN** (Islam *et al.*), a “Fast Underwater Image Enhancement GAN.” FUnIE-GAN’s architecture is a lightweight encoder–decoder CNN paired with a discriminator. It uses a **multi-term loss function** that includes pixel-wise reconstruction loss and adversarial loss, as well as penalties targeting **color, texture, and style** preservation. By formulating a composite objective (e.g., enforcing global color correctness and local texture sharpness), it aims to ensure the output is not only accurate but also natural-looking. An important feature of FUnIE-GAN is its ability to train on **both paired and unpaired data**. In a fully supervised setting, it uses direct image pairs; in unpaired mode, it leverages techniques from CycleGAN to learn enhancement as a style translation problem. The authors report that FUnIE-GAN achieves real-time inference on modest hardware (tens of frames per second on a Jetson GPU), making it practical for robotic applications. The outputs from GAN models like FUnIE-GAN generally have **higher contrast and more vivid colors** than those from pure CNNs, thanks to the adversarial training that discourages the overly smooth solutions. On the downside, GANs can introduce their own artifacts – e.g., slight checkerboard textures or speckles – if the discriminator drives the generator to hallucinate details. Ensuring stability and **preventing mode collapse** (where the generator produces a limited variety of outputs) requires careful design. For underwater GANs, one risk is that the model might learn a **stylistic color shift** that looks subjectively good to the discriminator but is not faithful to the true scene (since ground truth is unknown). To mitigate this, some works incorporate **cycle-consistency** (as in CycleGAN) or **dual discriminators**. A recent approach, **PUGAN** by Cong *et al.* combines a GAN with a physics model: it includes a **parameter estimation subnet** that learns the attenuation and backscatter coefficients, uses those to perform a preliminary physics-based restoration, and feeds that into a two-stream GAN generator. The GAN then refines the result, and dual discriminators enforce that output is both **photorealistic in style and consistent in content**. By inheriting the advantages of model-based methods (better generality across conditions) and GANs (better visuals), PUGAN achieved state-of-art results. This trend of integrating physics into deep models indicates that **pure learning** may be insufficient; providing constraints or insights from the underwater formation model can guide the network to avoid implausible restorations. Still, GAN or hybrid models require careful tuning of the loss balance – too much reliance on the discriminator can cause **over-saturated colors or invented textures**, whereas too little reverts to blurry CNN-like results.

Most recently, **Transformer-based networks** have entered the field of underwater enhancement. Vision Transformers and hybrid Transformer-CNN models can capture **long-range dependencies and global context** better than typical CNNs, which is attractive for underwater scenes where global color balance (e.g., ensuring the water background has consistent color) matters. Chen *et al.* and others introduced a **U-Shape Transformer** architecture for UIE, which replaces or augments the convolutional layers with self-attention layers to model relationships across the entire image. For instance, the U-Shape Transformer by Li *et al.* (2022) incorporates a *channel-wise multi-scale feature fusion transformer* and *spatial global modeling* module to handle both local details and global color cast. These models achieve excellent quantitative results on benchmark datasets – in fact, in one comparative study, a U-Transformer outperformed prior GAN and CNN methods in SSIM and color accuracy. The advantage is that transformers, by their self-attention mechanism, can implicitly learn something akin to depth: e.g., a pixel in a darker bluish region can “attend” to other regions to infer that it is likely background water that needs more correction. Additionally, Transformers can utilize large **pre-training**. Qu *et al.* (2023) proposed **UIEFormer**, which builds on a dehazing Transformer backbone pre-trained on natural images. By starting from a model already adept at removing haze in air, and then fine-tuning with a small underwater dataset, they achieved strong results even without extremely large underwater training data. This is crucial because curating large paired underwater datasets is difficult. UIEFormer also employs specialized components (e.g., a tailored upsampling strategy to avoid checkerboard artifacts, and a combination of per-pixel, perceptual, and color losses to guide training). The formal, **academically rigorous tone** of these transformer approaches belies a practical point: they are complex and computationally heavy. Self-attention layers scale quadratically with image size, so many designs use downsampling or mixed CNN-transformer layers to manage complexity. Even so, training such models demands significant computational resources, and they risk **overfitting** if the training set is not diverse enough (hence the need for pre-training or data augmentation). When they do work well, Transformer-based methods produce very balanced enhancements – often **preserving edge details sharply while globally correcting color** – thanks to their ability to handle both local and global features. A failure mode to watch is that, if forced to train on a small dataset from scratch, a transformer might **learn spurious correlations** (e.g., always make the image very blue because the training set had mostly shallow-water scenes) more so than a CNN, due to its greater capacity. Ensuring **proper regularization or use of extensive data** is therefore essential.


Diffusion models represent a cutting-edge advancement in image restoration, particularly in underwater image enhancement. Diffusion-based generative models, also known as score-based models, iteratively refine random noise into realistic images, effectively learning the data distribution of the target domain. They have demonstrated remarkable performance in tasks like inpainting and denoising, making them a natural choice for addressing underwater image degradation. However, diffusion models typically require training on large corpora of clean images, which are lacking in the underwater domain, especially for diverse scenes at varying depths. Bar-Nathan et al. addressed this challenge in their ECCV 2024 work "Osmosis" by proposing a novel strategy: instead of collecting clean underwater images, they trained an unconditional diffusion prior on RGB-D images of natural scenes in air, using RGB-D datasets captured by depth sensors or LiDAR as surrogates for what the world looks like without water. The diffusion model learns a prior over joint color and depth space, capturing correlations between texture and scene geometry. For a given underwater image, Osmosis uses the physical underwater image formation model as a guidance mechanism during the diffusion sampling process. It generates candidate clean images that, when passed through the underwater model with estimated depth or attenuation, approximate the input image. By iteratively refining these samples to satisfy both the diffusion prior and the physical model constraints, the system converges on a restored image that matches the underwater observation while resembling a realistic in-air scene. This approach achieves state-of-the-art results without training on underwater data, and the incorporation of depth information is shown to be critical, as color alone cannot disambiguate many underwater distortions. The Bayesian nature of diffusion models allows them to model uncertainty and avoid over-smoothing, preserving realistic textures and details where CNNs might fail. Furthermore, the inclusion of a physical model enforces cycle-consistency, ensuring the restored image remains physically plausible when reprojected through the underwater process. Nonetheless, diffusion models are computationally expensive, often requiring dozens or hundreds of iterations per image. Osmosis, for example, is far from real-time. It also relies on accurate estimates of underwater parameters—such as depth and attenuation—which are difficult to obtain robustly. A notable limitation is that while Osmosis effectively restores color, it does not meaningfully address blurring. Experiments indicate that its contribution lies primarily in “colorization,” restoring visually plausible color and texture, but offering limited improvement in edge sharpness or structural clarity. Thus, Osmosis is better suited for color correction than deblurring. Moreover, while the RGB-D prior captures general scene statistics, it lacks exposure to uniquely underwater content like corals or marine snow. Although Osmosis performs well on many underwater scenes due to analogs like rocks and fish, it may misinterpret truly novel structures, producing a generic “terrestrial” look. Other recent efforts, such as UIEDP, adopt plug-and-play strategies, integrating diffusion priors trained on natural images into enhancement networks. These studies collectively underscore the growing promise of diffusion models as powerful priors for underwater image enhancement.

In summary, deep learning methods have rapidly advanced underwater imaging, from the first CNNs that simply learned a direct mapping, to sophisticated GAN and Transformer models that incorporate perceptual and global reasoning. Each comes with trade-offs: CNNs (with possibly multi-branch inputs) are efficient and straightforward but may miss global coherence; GANs improve realism but can hallucinate; Transformers capture global context but require data or pre-training to avoid overfitting. Diffusion models represent a new frontier in this progression. As generative Bayesian methods, they excel at handling uncertainty and preserving fine detail by sampling from learned distributions. When coupled with physical models, such as in Osmosis, they can achieve cycle-consistent restoration that respects both perceptual realism and physical plausibility. However, diffusion models are computationally intensive and currently unsuitable for real-time deployment. Moreover, while they restore color convincingly, they often fall short in resolving underwater blur, acting more like colorization tools than full deblurring solutions. Despite this, diffusion approaches hold significant promise as strong priors for underwater image enhancement. All these methods, to varying extents, are constrained by the data used to train them – which leads us to the importance of synthetic data generation and depth information in training, as discussed next.
## Depth Cues and Depth Estimation in Enhancement

**Depth (distance of the scene points from the camera)** plays a pivotal role in underwater image degradation: the farther the light travels, the more it is absorbed and scattered. Incorporating depth information or estimates into the enhancement process can therefore greatly improve results. Traditional restoration methods implicitly use depth by estimating a **transmission map** (which is directly related to distance) – e.g., dark channel or red channel methods essentially derive a rough depth map as an intermediate. However, these estimates are often noisy or inaccurate in complex scenes, as discussed earlier. In recent years, researchers have sought to use **explicit depth priors or sensors** to guide enhancement. On the hardware side, one approach is to obtain depth via stereo cameras or structured light in the underwater environment. For instance, the **Sea-thru algorithm** by Akkaynak and Treibitz uses **stereo vision to get a precise range map for each pixel**, then plugs that into a revised physics model to recover true scene colors. By knowing the distance of objects and calibrating the water’s attenuation coefficients, Sea-thru can **accurately remove the color cast and haze** in a physically grounded way. The results are quantitatively excellent – Sea-thru produces images that match in-air photographs of the same objects in color fidelity. The limitation, of course, is the requirement of **RGB-D input** (Sea-thru obtains depth via structure-from-motion on multiple images, effectively needing multi-view input). This restricts its use to scenarios where such data can be collected. Moreover, Sea-thru assumes a spatially uniform water column between the camera and object; if there are local turbidity variations or if part of the scene is in different water (like an interface of murky and clear water), even depth won’t save the day because the model itself would need spatial variation. Nonetheless, Sea-thru demonstrated the upper bound of what’s possible when depth is known: it **eliminates water effects without the ambiguity** that single-image methods face. It also highlighted that many *apparent* color biases in single-image results were due to depth misestimation – when depth is correct, the reconstruction is more reliable.

When actual depth sensors are unavailable, the alternative is to **estimate depth from the single underwater image** as a prior to enhancement. This is an ill-posed problem (even more so than in-air monocular depth, since contrast is low and textures are faint), but several strategies exist. Earlier methods like **UDCP** provided a proxy depth map (proportional to light extinction) from the dark channel assumption. **IBLA** gave a better depth map by combining blur and color loss cues. Yet these are heuristic and can fail if assumptions break (e.g., an object that is naturally blue might be mistaken for being far away). Learning-based depth estimation tailored to underwater images is now being explored. One interesting recent work by Huang *et al.* proposes a **Depth-Guided Two-Stage Network (UVZ)** for UIE. In the first stage, a dedicated **depth estimation network** predicts a depth map from the underwater image. They even introduce an auxiliary loss (perhaps by using synthetic data or pseudo-ground-truth depth) to ensure this depth map is reliable. In the second stage, the enhancement network uses the predicted depth to apply different processing to **near vs far regions**. Essentially, the network mimics the human intuition: close-by areas need less color correction (since light hasn’t traveled far) but maybe some de-noising, whereas far areas need heavy dehazing and color boost. The depth map allows the network to **“zoom” adaptively** – hence the name “Underwater Variable Zoom” – attending to different scales or levels of restoration for different depths. Experiments show this yields better contrast and detail, because the network no longer has to find a one-size-fits-all filter for the whole image. The assumption here is that one can get a reasonably accurate depth map from stage one. If the depth prediction is poor, it could even harm the result (the network might wrongly sharpen a far-away blurred object thinking it is near, etc.). To improve depth prediction, such methods often rely on **synthetic underwater data** where true depth is known, or on leveraging pre-trained depth models from land images adjusted to underwater domains. There is also research on **estimating depth from focus** (using the camera’s autofocus and blur) or using polarization cues, but those typically need special setups. In software, a notable approach is **WaterGAN’s implicit depth learning**: when Li *et al.* trained their end-to-end WaterGAN correction network, they found it implicitly learned a coarse depth map as part of its internal representation. This happened because the network had to disentangle depth-dependent color changes to perform color correction. This suggests that even without explicit supervision, deep models *could* learn depth to some extent. However, providing an explicit depth input or loss can greatly ease the task.

Another way depth is used is in **adaptive filtering**. Some enhancement algorithms modulate their parameters based on an estimated depth. For example, an underwater dehazing might increase the amount of contrast stretching with depth. This is a simpler form than the two-stage networks – essentially a depth-dependent gain control. Such an approach requires a formula or pre-estimated map to tie into the enhancement algorithm. A limitation is that if depth estimation is slightly off, the whole enhancement can be off in those regions – e.g., **a halo can appear around an object** if its edges are assigned the wrong depth and thus over- or under-enhanced relative to their surroundings. Depth-estimation itself in single images can fail in featureless regions (water background with no texture) – those often get a default “far” value, which may cause overcorrection (noise amplification) in a uniform blue water patch.

In summary, depth information – whether from dedicated sensors, stereo, or intelligent estimation – is extremely valuable for UIE. It **provides the missing link** in many methods, turning an ill-posed problem into a well-posed one. We see two extremes: physics-based methods like Sea-thru that **require external depth but then succeed brilliantly**, and deep learning methods that **try to predict depth on the fly** to get some of that benefit. The latter is gaining traction as depth estimation networks improve. UnderwaterEnhanceNet, as we will discuss, follows this trend by integrating depth cues directly into its enhancement process, ensuring that the network’s treatment of an image is depth-aware at every location.

# Related Work

## Datasets in Related Image Restoration Fields

**Motion Deblurring:** Obtaining paired sharp and blurred images is challenging, so researchers have resorted to high-speed cameras and synthetic blurring. For example, the **GoPro dataset** introduced by Nah et al. was created by recording videos at 240 fps and averaging consecutive frames to simulate motion blur. It contains over 2,100 blurred/sharp image pairs at 720p resolution. Similarly, **HIDE (Human-aware Image Deblurring)** provided 8,422 pairs of blurred and sharp images with people in the scene. HIDE was captured with a high-speed camera and diverse motions, plus it includes human bounding box annotations for each image. These datasets simulate realistic camera shakes and object motion by integrating multiple short-exposure frames into a single blurred image, following a *known point spread function (PSF)* rather than physically taking long exposures. Older benchmarks synthetically convolved sharp images with preset blur kernels, but the GoPro and HIDE datasets were a breakthrough in creating large-scale training data with true alignments. More recently, **RealBlur** (Rim et al., ECCV 2020) introduced a *hardware capture* solution: they built a beam-splitter camera rig to **simultaneously capture a short-exposure “sharp” image and a long-exposure “blurred” image** of the same scene. This yielded 4,738 real blurred/sharp pairs (RealBlur-J/JPEG and RealBlur-R/RAW) for training and evaluation. The availability of precise ground truth in RealBlur comes at the cost of a complex capture system, whereas high-frame-rate synthetic datasets (GoPro, HIDE) trade off some realism for quantity and diversity. In summary, motion deblurring datasets have been constructed by **frame accumulation (simulated blur), dynamic scenes captured at high FPS, and dual-camera setups for real blur**, since truly capturing the exact blur and ground truth simultaneously is inherently difficult.

**Rain, Fog, and Haze Removal:** Adverse weather restoration has also relied on simulation. For **image deraining**, early datasets like Rain12 and **Rain100L/H** introduced by Yang et al. were purely synthetic, adding streak-like noise to clean images. **Rain100H** (heavy rain) contains 100 particularly challenging synthetic rainy images generated by overlaying many streaks at varying angles and intensities on clean photographs. The rain generation often uses a *streak appearance model* (e.g. Gaussian drop shapes convolved with motion blur) without depth variation, meaning streaks are uniformly applied across the image. **RainCityscapes**, proposed by Hu et al. (CVPR 2019), improved realism by leveraging the Cityscapes driving dataset: they took real street images and added rain streaks **with physical modeling based on scene depth**. Using the Cityscapes disparity maps, they rendered rain of different densities and even included slight fog to mimic heavy rainfall. This rendering-based approach accounts for the fact that distant objects should be more obscured by rain/fog than near ones. RainCityscapes thus provides paired clean and rainy images where the rain is **depth-aware and photorealistic**. Similarly for **dehazing**, the **RESIDE** benchmark (Li et al. 2018) introduced a large corpus of paired clear/hazy images generated by the standard atmospheric scattering model. RESIDE’s *Indoor Training Set (ITS)* was built from indoor RGB-D images (e.g. NYU-Depth) by applying the **Koschmieder’s law** \$I(x)=J(x)t(x)+A(1-t(x))\$, with transmission \$t(x)=\exp(-\beta d(x))\$ computed from the depth map. The *Outdoor Training Set (OTS)* similarly applied a haze model to outdoor images using estimated depth (e.g. from the Make3D range dataset). By varying the global airlight \$A\$ and scattering coefficient \$\beta\$, RESIDE synthesized over **?** 100,000 hazy images with known ground truth. Evaluation sets like **SOTS** (Synthetic Objective Testing Set) were held out for benchmarking. In parallel, Sakaridis et al. created **Foggy Cityscapes** by taking Cityscapes images and algorithmically adding fog using the provided semi-dense depth maps. They generated 20,550 foggy images with three levels of fog density for each original image, producing paired data for semantic segmentation and dehazing. In all these cases, the underlying physical model (rain streak rendering, light scattering in haze) is used to degrade real clean images. **Paired real data is extremely hard to get** (you cannot have the same outdoor scene simultaneously clear and hazy), so simulation with depth or statistical overlays has become the norm. Recently, a few works have attempted *time-lapse pairing* – e.g. capturing a scene before and during rain – but issues of illumination changes and exact alignment remain. Thus, synthetic datasets (Rain100, RainCityscapes, RESIDE, Foggy Cityscapes) continue to drive supervised deraining and dehazing research by providing large-scale paired training data created via **physical models of rain/fog superimposed on clear images**.

**Low-Light Enhancement and Denoising:** In low-light photography, the challenge is to get a clean “bright” ground truth for a dark input. The **LOL (Low-Light)** dataset by Wei et al. tackled this by capturing the same scene twice: once with normal exposure and once with low exposure (or by darkening the illumination), then aligning the images. LOL provides 500 real pairs of dark and well-lit images for learning enhancement. Another strategy is used in the **See-In-the-Dark (SID)** dataset: Chen et al. captured raw images in near-total darkness with a short exposure, and the ground truth was a long exposure of the same static scene. By shooting RAW images and careful alignment on a tripod, SID amassed hundreds of paired examples of extreme low-light noise versus clean reference, despite the difficulty of capturing such scenes. For noise reduction, synthetic Gaussian noise training has been common (adding artificial noise to clean images), but recent real-noise datasets have emerged. The **Darmstadt Noise Dataset (DND)** (2017) and **SIDD** (Smartphone Image Denoising Dataset, 2019) both captured identical scenes twice with different ISO settings – one noisy (high ISO) and one nearly noise-free (low ISO) – to serve as input/ground truth pairs. While true one-to-one pairing is hard (even slight misalignment or scene change can spoil the pair), these datasets applied meticulous calibration and post-processing to ensure alignment. Notably, SIDD collected thousands of such pairs from five smartphone camera models in various lighting. In summary, low-light and denoising datasets either use **controlled capture with multiple exposure settings** (to get a bright reference for a dark image) or rely on **synthetically adding noise or gamma correction** to clean images. Real paired data is scarce, so hybrid approaches are used (e.g. SID’s raw capture approach or SIDD’s multi-shot averaging). These strategies allow supervised learning where otherwise one would lack ground truth in dark or noisy conditions.

**Rendered Synthetic Data for Vision:** Another line of dataset construction uses fully **virtual 3D rendering to generate paired data** for tasks like optical flow, depth, or segmentation. The **MPI Sintel** dataset (Butler et al. 2012) is a prime example: it took an open-source animated short film (“Sintel”) and re-rendered the scenes to produce a dataset with ground-truth optical flow, multi-view stereo disparity, and segmentation. Sintel provides both a “clean” render and a “final” render with motion blur, atmospheric effects, and defocus, thus yielding paired data (clean vs. degraded) in addition to labels for each pixel’s motion. **Virtual KITTI** (Gaidon et al. 2016) cloned real KITTI driving scenes in a game engine (Unity) to produce *photo-realistic* synthetic videos with ground truth for object detection, tracking, depth, and flow. It consists of 21,260 images across multiple virtual worlds, with each scene rendered under multiple conditions (e.g. rain, fog, different camera angles). By having exact 3D knowledge, Virtual KITTI can provide **pixel-perfect annotation** (segmentation masks, depth maps, flow fields) that real KITTI cannot. **BlenderProc** (Denninger et al. 2019) further streamlined this idea by offering an open-source pipeline on top of Blender for procedural dataset generation. With BlenderProc, one can input 3D object models and obtain rendered images along with **automatic ground-truth annotations (bounding boxes, depth, surface normals)**. This has been used in domains like 6D object pose (e.g. generating thousands of scenes of objects in random poses for training detectors). In optical flow, **FlyingChairs** and **FlyingThings3D** are popular synthetic benchmarks. FlyingChairs (Dosovitskiy et al. 2015) was created by compositing cut-out images of chairs moving across random backgrounds; it contains 22,872 image pairs with ground-truth flow vectors known from the applied affine transformations. FlyingThings3D (Mayer et al. 2016) extended this to 3D: it consists of everyday 3D objects (chairs, tables, etc.) “flying” along random trajectories in a 3D scene, rendered with realistic lighting, yielding a large dataset with ground truth optical flow, stereo disparity, and even scene flow (3D motion). These fully synthetic datasets demonstrate a key strategy for supervised learning: **if real paired data is unobtainable, simulate the world**. By using game engines or ray-tracing renderers, one can generate unlimited paired examples (image + ground truth annotation) for problems like depth estimation, flow, or deblurring – albeit with the perpetual caveat of a *domain gap* between rendered and real images. Nonetheless, the success of models trained on Sintel or Virtual KITTI and fine-tuned on real data shows the power of this approach. The best practices from these efforts include: simulating realistic textures and lighting, applying physics-based degradation (motion blur, noise) to mimic real sensor effects, and providing multitudes of **modalities** (RGB, depth, segmentation, etc.) as supervision. We will see these same principles applied in the underwater domain.

## Underwater Image Enhancement Datasets and Synthesis

Early work on underwater image enhancement lacked large paired datasets, since capturing an underwater scene “with and without distortion” is essentially impossible in situ. Researchers therefore pursued two main routes: collecting real underwater images with **approximate reference corrections**, and generating synthetic underwater images from known pristine images via **physical models of light propagation**.

**Real-World Benchmarks with Reference Images:** A notable example is the **UIEB (Underwater Image Enhancement Benchmark)** by Li et al.. UIEB consists of 950 real underwater photographs; for 890 of these, the authors provide a **high-quality reference image** that serves as the ground truth for enhancement. These reference images were obtained through a comprehensive human study: for each raw underwater image, they either manually retouched it or chose the best result among several enhancement algorithms, effectively establishing a pseudo-ground-truth by expert consensus. (The remaining 60 images were deemed too challenging to assign any satisfactory reference and are included only for qualitative testing.) UIEB was the first large-scale underwater benchmark with “paired” data in the sense of an input and a desired output, enabling quantitative evaluation of enhancement algorithms. Following a similar spirit, **EUVP (Enhancing Underwater Visual Perception)** was introduced by Islam et al. alongside their FUnIE-GAN model. EUVP contains a *paired set* and an *unpaired set* of images, all collected from ocean dives with different cameras and conditions. The **paired subset** of EUVP provides underwater images labeled as “poor quality” together with a “good quality” version of the same scene. In practice, the good-quality reference might be obtained by either adjusting camera settings (e.g. turning on a flash or light, or moving closer to reduce distortion) or by applying an enhancement algorithm that was verified to improve perceptual quality. EUVP’s unpaired subset is simply a large collection of poor and clean underwater images for methods that don’t require direct alignment. Importantly, EUVP was curated to support *both* **paired training (supervised)** and **unpaired training (CycleGAN-style)** for underwater enhancement. It contains on the order of 8K paired instances and a similar number of unpaired examples. Both UIEB and EUVP reflect an attempt to leverage real imagery (thus containing genuine underwater light effects) while still providing a target output for learning. The drawback is that these “ground truths” are **subjective** or approximated – e.g. UIEB’s references maximize human visual preference, which may vary, and EUVP’s paired images are not always exact pixel-wise alignments but rather notionally the same scene in better clarity. Nonetheless, they have been widely used to train and benchmark deep models (the UIEB paper itself introduced a baseline CNN called *Water-Net* trained on these references). Another real-data approach is the **Sea-thru** dataset by Akkaynak and Treibitz (CVPR 2019). Rather than providing an enhanced image by manual adjustment, Sea-thru provides **raw underwater images along with depth maps for each pixel**. The authors collected stereo images of underwater scenes (1100 images across 5 sites) and computed a dense range map via structure-from-motion and multi-view stereo. They also placed color calibration charts in the scene to measure true colors at known distances. With this information, they introduced a physics-based algorithm that **removes the water effects** (absorption and scattering) from the image, essentially reconstructing how the scene would look in clear water at a standard distance. Sea-thru’s contribution is twofold: (1) a dataset with ground-truth depth for real underwater scenes, enabling quantitative evaluation of image restoration via physical metrics, and (2) a novel model of underwater image formation to serve as the basis for synthesis/removal. While Sea-thru does not provide a direct “clean image” for each underwater photo (since such a thing would require draining the ocean!), the depth maps allow any method that *assumes* a physics model to compute a corrected image. Thus, one can generate a reference by applying Beer–Lambert law inversely if the water parameters are known. In practice, Sea-thru showed that by using the **Beer–Lambert attenuation law** (exponential decay of light with distance) for each color channel and knowing per-pixel distance, one can remove a large portion of the blue-green cast and haze from underwater photos. Sea-thru’s dataset has been valuable for evaluating how well learning-based methods recover physically accurate colors (e.g. checking color charts in the output). It represents an intermediate approach between real and synthetic: the data is real, but the availability of depth acts as a surrogate for ground-truth in training physics-based enhancement models.

**Synthetic Underwater Data via Physical Models:** Alongside real-image benchmarks, researchers have synthesized underwater image pairs by applying underwater optics models to known clean images. A prominent example is the work of Li **et al.** on **UWCNN (Underwater Color Correction CNN)**. They noted that different water types (clear ocean, turbid coastal, etc.) have different light attenuation coefficients. Using the classic **Jerlov water type classification**, they defined 5 types for open ocean (I, IA, IB, II, III) and 5 types for coastal water (1, 3, 5, 7, 9) with pre-specified wavelength-dependent attenuation coefficients for red, green, blue light. They then took an existing RGB-D dataset of *in-air* scenes (NYU-V2 indoor dataset) and for each RGB image with depth, they **simulated how it would look underwater** at various distances and water types. Specifically, they used the **Beer–Lambert law** for direct attenuation and a parametric model for backscatter (veiling light) to generate synthetic underwater images:

$I_c(x) = J_c(x)\, \exp(-\beta_c \, d(x)) + B_c \big(1 - \exp(-\beta_c \, d(x))\big),$

where \$J\_c\$ is the clean image, \$d(x)\$ is the depth, \$\beta\_c\$ is the attenuation coefficient for color channel \$c\$, and \$B\_c\$ is the homogeneous background light color. By plugging in \$\beta\$ values from Jerlov water type tables for each channel and randomizing the ambient light \$B\_c\$, they created **10 synthetic underwater versions** of each NYU image (one for each water type). In total, Li et al. generated a training set of 5,000 synthetic underwater images (1000 original images \$\times\$ 5 random renders each) and a validation set of \~2,500, each paired with the original clean image as ground truth. The result was the first large paired dataset covering a *diverse range of turbidity and color casts*. They trained separate UWCNN models specialized for each water type and showed significantly improved color restoration when the water type is known. This approach explicitly uses **physical priors (absorption coefficients)** and produces data where the alignment is perfect (since the underwater image is a direct pixelwise transform of the clean image). Around the same time, **WaterGAN** (Li, Skinner et al. 2017) took a different generative approach. WaterGAN uses a **GAN pipeline to synthesize underwater images** from in-air images and depth maps. It inputs a true RGB-D pair (e.g. an image of a land scene and its depth) into a generator network that applies an underwater style learned from unlabeled underwater photos. The generator essentially implements a learned distortion comprising *wavelength-dependent attenuation (using Beer–Lambert)* and *additive scatter* with some randomness, and it is trained adversarially to make the output look statistically like real underwater images. WaterGAN was **unsupervised** in that it did not require paired underwater/clean images to train – instead, it learned to degrade clean images into underwater-like images by matching the distribution of real underwater data. Using this, the authors generated a large synthetic dataset: each clean RGB-D image yields a synthetic underwater image, and the original clean image is the ground truth for color correction. In their work, thousands of such pairs were produced to train a subsequent color-correction network. The key idea is that **depth maps plus physics (even if learned via GAN) enable generating realistic underwater images with known ground truth**. WaterGAN’s output images included effects of **color attenuation and haze** that mimic the real samples and even some texture noise, while still retaining the correspondence to the input image content. A number of other works followed on physically-motivated synthesis: e.g. **UW‐Syn** (2018) and **SyreaNet** (2021) proposed improved physics-based simulators that start from in-air images, simulate the attenuation using known or random water parameters, and sometimes even add **particulates or caustic lighting effects** for realism. In general, these synthetic approaches rely on the **optical imaging model** of Jaffe-McGlamery: direct attenuation (Beer–Lambert law) plus forward-scatter and backscatter components. By sampling parameters like water clarity (attenuation length) and ambient light color, one can generate many versions of a clean image under different “water types.” The advantage is full pairing; the challenge is ensuring the synthetic images cover the visual complexity of real underwater scenes (e.g. natural light patterns, shadows, or flora/fauna content). Nonetheless, datasets like UWCNN’s NYU-based synthesis and WaterGAN’s GAN-based synthesis have provided valuable training data for supervised underwater enhancement and depth estimation. These synthetic datasets explicitly model **color absorption and scattering** – for instance, using known Jerlov curves for clear vs. turbid water – which means algorithms trained on them can, in theory, learn the *physical inversion* of those effects.

**Summary of Early Work:** By 2020, the field had access to a mix of: moderate-sized real image datasets with subjective reference images (UIEB, EUVP), fully synthetic paired data based on physics (UWCNN, etc.), and real data with depth calibration (Sea-thru). Each comes with trade-offs. Real images have uncontrolled content but only approximate ground truth; purely synthetic images have perfect ground truth but may miss some real-world complexity. This backdrop set the stage for more advanced synthetic datasets that attempt to combine realism and ground truth at scale.

## Advanced Synthetic Underwater Datasets

**VAROS (ICCV 2021):** Recent efforts like **VAROS** have leveraged modern rendering engines to produce highly realistic underwater data with comprehensive ground truth. VAROS (Zwilgmeyer et al. 2021) is a synthetic underwater environment built in Blender, inspired by autonomous driving simulators like CARLA. The authors created large 3D underwater scenes (sea floor terrains, rocks, and objects) and simulated an underwater vehicle with controllable motion and lighting. Critically, the rendering is done with **physical ray tracing that accounts for volumetric light scattering**. They implement a water volume with particulates and attenuation properties, so that the rendered images exhibit realistic haze, light shafts, and color loss as distance increases – closely following the physics of real underwater optics. VAROS provides **multiple synchronized modalities**: each frame comes with the standard RGB image, a corresponding image *without water or lighting effects* (essentially the same scene rendered under uniform light and no attenuation), plus pixel-perfect **depth maps, surface normal maps, and even inertial sensor readings**. The inclusion of a “no water, uniform light” image for each frame is especially useful – that image serves as a **true reference** showing what the scene would look like in ideal clear conditions. Thus, VAROS inherently provides paired data for enhancement: the underwater image and its clear counterpart are exactly aligned. The first version of VAROS contains \~4,713 images (frames) at 10 FPS from a simulated submersible trajectory, and further sequences are in preparation. Importantly, the simulation can produce not just static image pairs but **video sequences** with ground-truth camera poses and IMU readings, enabling research on video deblurring or SLAM in underwater settings. By using Blender’s Cycles engine, VAROS achieves a high level of photorealism (global illumination, caustics on the seabed, realistic light fall-off). The water properties in VAROS are configurable; the authors mention using known models like the Petzold scattering phase function or Jerlov water types to adjust turbidity, though approximations are still needed for some optical aspects. In summary, VAROS demonstrates a **game-engine-generated dataset** that marries physical accuracy with the full ground truth of simulation. It is to underwater vision what Sintel/Virtual KITTI were to land vision – a way to generate unlimited data where everything is known. Early results show that networks trained on VAROS can be effectively tested on real underwater data for tasks like depth estimation and image restoration, with domain gap mitigation via domain randomization (since VAROS can randomize water clarity, lighting, etc.). VAROS is a **supervised learning treasure trove**: it provides **pixel-aligned pairs of degraded and clear images, depth, normals, and even sonar/LiDAR simulations** for each scene. This represents a significant step beyond earlier synthetic efforts that only output the degraded image.

**Atlantis (CVPR 2024):** While VAROS relies on explicit 3D modeling and rendering, the **Atlantis** dataset (Zhang et al. 2024) explores a complementary approach using generative AI. Atlantis is introduced as a pipeline for generating **photorealistic underwater images from terrestrial images and accurate depth maps using latent diffusion models**. The motivation is to address the domain gap: models trained on land images (for depth or detection) perform poorly underwater due to color and scattering differences. Yet collecting paired underwater data is hard. Atlantis tackles this by taking *in-air RGB-D data* (for example, an image with its depth from KITTI or NYU) and using a **Stable Diffusion-based generative model** to create a brand new underwater scene that **respects the given depth map**. In essence, they train a **Depth-to-Underwater diffusion model (a specialized ControlNet)** that takes a depth map as conditioning (and possibly a text prompt describing an underwater setting) and outputs a realistic underwater image matching that geometry. This approach is different from style transfer: rather than simply tinting or overlaying effects on an existing image, the diffusion model can generate **entirely new underwater content (“vivid non-existent underwater scenes”)** while ensuring structures align with the provided depth. For training this model, Atlantis prepared a triplet dataset of *Underwater Image, Depth, and descriptive Text* – likely by collecting a large set of real underwater images with either estimated depth or known ranges (perhaps leveraging Sea-thru or other stereo data) and captions. The result is a generative model that understands both the physics (through depth conditioning) and the appearance of underwater environments (through the diffusion prior). Using this model, the authors generated the **Atlantis dataset**, which consists of pairs of **synthetic underwater images and their ground-truth depth maps** (the depth maps are originally from land datasets, hence “accurate terrestrial depth”). These image/depth pairs were then used to train and significantly improve a monocular depth estimator for real underwater scenes. The Atlantis images exhibit a high level of realism – the diffusion model naturally learned to incorporate **light attenuation with distance, color shifts, and hazy scattering** in a visually plausible way. Tiny details like floating particulate matter, shafts of light, or sea snow can be synthesized by the model, which are phenomena difficult to model analytically but easily learned from data. According to the authors, **Atlantis yields depth prediction models that outperform those trained on terrestrial data when evaluated on real underwater images**, showing the value of this synthetic data. They also demonstrate using the improved depth as a cue for underwater image enhancement, indicating a cascading benefit. In summary, Atlantis represents a **diffusion-driven data synthesis**: by fusing *deep generative priors* with *physics conditioning (depth)*, it produces extremely realistic underwater images with automatic ground truth. This is a novel direction for dataset creation – it does not require manual 3D content creation (as VAROS does) and can scale with the availability of terrestrial RGB-D data. One limitation is that the scenes, while realistic, are *imaginary* and not exact reproductions of real locations (the geometry comes from land data). Nonetheless, Atlantis achieves a remarkable level of realism in underwater appearance, covering the spectrum of **scattering and absorption effects**. It implicitly learns the **Beer–Lambert law** (since it sees depth input, it can attenuate colors accordingly) and the look of **suspended particles and forward-scatter** (since these are present in training images and the diffusion model can reproduce such textures). Thus, Atlantis stands at the cutting edge of synthetic dataset generation – combining **AI-based content creation with physical correctness** – to yield high-fidelity paired data for supervised learning.

## Blender-Based Underwater Dataset (Proposed)

Having surveyed the evolution of dataset construction strategies, we now describe the approach taken in this work, which aims to combine the strengths of prior methods. We introduce a new synthetic underwater dataset generated through **Blender-based full-scene rendering with explicit physical modeling of absorption and scattering**. In our pipeline, complete 3D scenes are created (including terrain, objects, and textures) and then rendered twice: once under normal clear conditions, and once with an underwater medium inserted between the camera and the scene. The underwater rendering uses volumetric shaders that implement the **wavelength-dependent Beer–Lambert attenuation** (to produce realistic color fall-off for red, green, blue channels) and per-volume scattering to create turbidity/haze similar to the Jaffe-McGlamery model. By doing this in a controlled 3D environment, we obtain **truly paired images**: for every synthetic underwater image, we have a ground-truth clean image of the identical scene (same camera pose, same lighting except without attenuation). We also simulate **camera motion and motion blur** in the underwater renders – for example, by moving the camera along a trajectory and using Blender’s motion blur settings or frame averaging to produce realistic blur. The result is a dataset that provides multi-level supervision: we have pairs of **blurred underwater images and sharp underwater images**, as well as pairs of sharp underwater vs. clear in-air images. This enables training networks in a cascaded or joint fashion (first to deblur, then to color-correct/dehaze). The **turbidity is controllable**: we generate the data under different water types (clear, medium, very turbid) by adjusting the scattering coefficient in the volumetric shader, akin to the Jerlov classification. Likewise, we vary lighting conditions (ambient light color, presence of a sunlight directional source, etc.) to incorporate diverse visibility ranges. Because everything is synthetic, we can produce **limitless data and perfectly aligned ground truth**. We ensure the content of the scenes is varied – including coral reefs, wrecks, moving marine life – to prevent overfitting to simple textures. Our approach is significant in that it **bridges the gap between purely physics-based synthesis and game-engine realism**. Unlike UIEB or EUVP, our dataset provides a deterministic ground truth (not a subjective enhancement). And unlike prior synthetic sets that address either color alone (UWCNN, WaterGAN) or geometry alone (Sea-thru depth maps), our work addresses **multiple degradation factors simultaneously**: *both* motion blur and underwater color loss. This is the first dataset to our knowledge that does so in a paired manner. In spirit, it parallels the best practices from other domains: for example, just as FlyingChairs added known transforms to introduce motion, we introduce known camera motion to produce blurred frames (with the sharp frame as ground truth). And similar to how Virtual KITTI and VAROS use a 3D engine for ground truth, we use Blender to get exact reference images and even pixel-wise depth if needed. The dataset is fully reproducible – we provide the Blender scene generation scripts and parameter sets, so researchers can extend or modify the data (e.g. generate more images or change water clarity). **In comparison to existing underwater datasets**, our Blender-generated data offers a unique combination of features: true pixel-aligned pairs, **controllable physics** (we know exactly the \$\beta\$ used for attenuation and can vary it), dynamic effects like blur which have not been present in others, and rich annotations (since we have the 3D scene, we can output depth or segmentation as additional supervision). This will allow training of models that tackle *underwater image restoration as a whole*, rather than isolating deblurring from color correction. By providing multi-factor degradation in a single framework, the dataset encourages networks that jointly learn deblurring, dehazing, and color correction – much like a real underwater robotics scenario where motion and poor visibility co-occur. In summary, our proposed dataset pushes the envelope by ensuring **ground-truth fidelity and physical realism** simultaneously. It is a direct response to the gap in current resources, combining ideas from VAROS (engine-based simulation) and Atlantis (high realism) with an added emphasis on motion blur and multi-condition training. We believe this dataset will significantly advance supervised underwater image enhancement, as it supplies the community with what was previously missing: a benchmark that is *both* physically grounded and comprehensive in simulating real-world underwater challenges.

Overall, the evolution from simple synthetic overlays (rain streaks, haze) to sophisticated renderings (VAROS, ours) and generative models (Atlantis) underscores a core theme: **when real paired data is infeasible, researchers construct it – via physics, rendering, or learning – to drive progress**. Our work continues this trajectory in the underwater domain, providing a new level of fidelity and breadth for future deep learning models.

## UnderwaterEnhanceNet in Context

Given the landscape above, **UnderwaterEnhanceNet** is designed as a direct response to the key limitations of existing methods. Traditional prior-based algorithms struggle without reliable depth cues and often yield suboptimal color correction (leaving residual tints or haze), while many CNN/GAN methods ignore physics and depth, risking inconsistent enhancement. Our proposed framework introduces an **adaptive depth fusion module** to explicitly condition the enhancement on distance. This means that, unlike most CNN or Transformer models which apply the same filters globally, UnderwaterEnhanceNet can **dynamically modulate its feature processing based on a pixel’s estimated depth**. Prior works have hinted at this – e.g., depth-guided filters in UVZ – but our model integrates depth signals throughout its architecture, ensuring both the color restoration and dehazing operations are depth-aware at each stage. By doing so, the network addresses the failure mode where uniform treatment causes over-enhancement of near regions or under-enhancement of far regions. In effect, **UnderwaterEnhanceNet treats shallow and deep parts of the image appropriately within one unified model**, something previously achieved only by multi-model or two-pass approaches.

Moreover, UnderwaterEnhanceNet employs a **dual-branch (dual-task) structure**, separating the concerns of **color correction** and **structure/detail enhancement**. This architectural choice is informed by the observation that correcting color (compensating wavelength attenuation) is a different problem from enhancing visibility (reducing scatter blur and improving contrast). Earlier networks tended to entangle these, which could lead to compromises – for instance, a network might slightly blur the image while removing color cast, because it trades off detail to minimize overall loss. In our design, one branch focuses on **chromatic restoration**: it attends to getting the hues and global illumination right (tackling the color distortion). The other branch focuses on **luminance and high-frequency information**: it aims to recover edges, textures, and contrast (counteracting structural degradation like blurring and low contrast). By training these branches with appropriate losses (e.g., a color fidelity loss on the color branch and a sharpness/contrast loss on the detail branch), each sub-network can specialize. They then **fuse their outputs**, guided by the depth module – for example, in very distant regions, the color branch’s output (which might also remove haze to some extent) is given more weight, whereas in closer regions, more of the detail branch can be used to sharpen fine features. This dual-task approach reduces typical artifacts: it avoids the **“sharp but wrong color” outcome of purely detail-trained models and the “correct color but blurry” outcome of purely color-trained models**. In essence, UnderwaterEnhanceNet **maintains both accurate color and crisp details** by not forcing one network to do everything.

Another critical aspect is the **training regimen with physically-plausible synthetic data and depth supervision**. We generate training pairs using an underwater image simulator that follows real physics (perhaps similar to the models used in prior physical methods, but extended to produce full images and depth). This ensures the network’s “ground truth” target truly represents a clear image of the same scene – giving it a consistent goal to aim for. Depth supervision (using the known depth from the simulator) is applied so that the depth fusion module learns to interpret its depth input meaningfully (or if the network predicts depth itself, it learns to predict it accurately). The benefit is twofold: (1) The network learns a **physically grounded mapping** – for instance, it learns that if the depth is large, the red channel needs significant boosting and contrast needs stretching, whereas if depth is small, minimal change is needed. This mirrors the Beer-Lambert law within the network’s function approximation. (2) Because the training data covers a range of water types and conditions (by varying parameters in simulation), UnderwaterEnhanceNet becomes **robust to different environments**. It is less likely to be confused by a novel scenario because it has effectively seen many possible variations during training (and learned the governing rule: depth). This approach addresses the **domain gap issue** that plagued earlier deep models – our network is not merely fitting a dataset, it’s internalizing a physical relationship (distance → attenuation) supported by the synthetic ground truth.

By situating UnderwaterEnhanceNet among related works, we can see it as an **amalgamation of the strengths** of those approaches: it carries forward the physics-based insistence on depth-awareness (as in UDCP, IBLA, Sea-thru) but realizes it through a learnable, adaptive module rather than a fixed prior. It leverages powerful CNN/Transformer layers to learn **nuanced mappings** that traditional methods couldn’t model (addressing complex color non-linearities and blur patterns), and does so in a multi-branch fashion to avoid the one-size-fits-all bottleneck. Compared to prior deep models, it explicitly avoids the “black box” criticism by injecting physical meaning (depth, color vs detail separation) into the architecture. Consequently, UnderwaterEnhanceNet **substantially mitigates common failure modes**: for example, it is far less prone to produce halos or inconsistent contrast because depth-guidance aligns the enhancement with actual scene geometry; it preserves edges and textures so the result is not plasticky or overly smooth; and it achieves natural color tones even under varying water conditions, without residual tints, thanks to its dedicated color pipeline and broad training data. In empirical evaluations, these design choices translate to images that not only score well on metrics but also *look* right – with realistic water colors and clear details, even for content at different distances in the scene. UnderwaterEnhanceNet thus represents a significant step forward, marrying the insights from decades of underwater imaging research with modern deep learning, to overcome the key shortcomings identified in the related work above.&#x20;
